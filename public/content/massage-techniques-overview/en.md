# Massage Techniques (Beyond Effleurage)

This topic covers the classic Swedish strokes beyond effleurage, plus the
advanced/therapeutic techniques commonly tested on the MBLEx. For
effleurage itself, see the **Effleurage** topic.

## Petrissage (Kneading)
- **What it is**: lifting, squeezing, and rolling soft tissue — like
  kneading dough.
- **Effects**: increases circulation, reduces muscle tension, assists
  lymphatic flow, helps break down adhesions.
- **Typical placement**: after effleurage has warmed the tissue, mid-session.
- **Avoid when**: severe varicosities, acute injury, active skin
  infection, hemophilia/bleeding disorders, uncontrolled hypertension.

## Friction (Circular / Cross-Fiber)
- **What it is**: focused, deeper pressure moved in small circles or
  across the grain of muscle fibers, generating localized heat.
- **Effects**: breaks down adhesions, increases local blood flow, helps
  realign collagen fibers during healing, reduces trigger point activity.
- **Typical placement**: mid-to-late session, on tissue that's already
  been warmed up.
- **Avoid when**: acute inflammation, bursitis, sharp pain during the
  technique; use extra caution near nerves or bony prominences.

## Tapotement (Percussion)
- **What it is**: rhythmic, brisk tapping, cupping, or hacking movements.
- **Effects**: stimulates nerves, energizes muscle, increases
  circulation — the opposite feeling of vibration.
- **Typical placement**: end of an invigorating or sports-massage
  routine.
- **Avoid when**: over the kidneys, bony prominences, varicosities,
  acute injury, clotting disorders, or with anxious clients (it can be
  overstimulating).

## Vibration
- **What it is**: fine, rapid shaking or trembling movement applied with
  the hands.
- **Effects**: sedates the nervous system, can reduce pain, relaxes tight
  tissue — opposite effect of tapotement.
- **Typical placement**: calming routines, later in a session,
  neuromuscular work.
- **Avoid when**: acute inflammation, unstable joints, severe neuropathy
  where the client can't reliably sense pressure.

## Compression
- **What it is**: rhythmic pressing into tissue (often with a flat hand
  or forearm), without gliding.
- **Effects**: increases circulation, warms the muscle, reduces
  hypertonicity (excess muscle tone).
- **Typical placement**: early-to-mid session; common in sports and Thai
  massage styles.
- **Avoid when**: acute injury, severe edema, clot risk (DVT), fragile
  skin, severe osteoporosis.

## Rocking / Rhythmic Mobilization
- **What it is**: gentle, rhythmic rocking of a body part or the whole
  body.
- **Effects**: decreases guarding (protective muscle tension), promotes
  relaxation, improves joint mobility — well suited for chronic pain.
- **Avoid when**: vertigo, severe osteoporosis, recent surgery, pain with
  movement.

## Skin Rolling
- **What it is**: lifting and rolling a fold of skin and superficial
  fascia between the fingers, without deep pressure.
- **Effects**: lifts and mobilizes fascia, breaks up adhesions, improves
  mobility.
- **Avoid when**: fragile skin, acute inflammation, severe edema, recent
  scars (under about 6 weeks old), varicosities.

## Myofascial Spreading / Traction
- **What it is**: slow, sustained spreading or pulling force on the
  fascia, usually with little or no lubricant so the hands can "grip" the
  tissue.
- **Effects**: lengthens fascia, improves posture, reduces fascial
  restrictions.
- **Avoid when**: acute inflammation, recent surgery; use caution with
  diagnosed connective-tissue disorders.

## Deep Tissue Stripping
- **What it is**: a slow, deep glide along the length of a muscle.
- **Effects**: lengthens muscle fibers, reduces chronic tension,
  deactivates trigger points.
- **Avoid when**: acute injury, uncontrolled hypertension, clot risk,
  neuropathy, or if the client can't tolerate the pressure.

## Static Pressure / Ischemic Compression
- **What it is**: sustained, focused pressure held on one point (often a
  trigger point), typically followed by a stretch.
- **Effects**: reduces trigger point activity and pain, can increase
  usable muscle length.
- **Avoid when**: vascular compromise, clotting disorders, uncontrolled
  hypertension, acute inflammation.

## Joint Mobilization / Passive Range of Motion
- **What it is**: the therapist moves a joint through its range of
  motion while the client stays relaxed.
- **Effects**: lubricates the joint, increases range of motion, reduces
  stiffness.
- **Avoid when**: recent surgery, hypermobility syndromes, an active
  arthritis flare, or if movement itself causes pain.

## Stretching (Passive or Active-Assisted)
- **What it is**: lengthening a muscle/tissue either with the therapist
  doing the work (passive) or the client actively participating
  (active-assisted).
- **Effects**: improves flexibility, lengthens tissue, increases range of
  motion.
- **Avoid when**: acute muscle strain, recent fractures, joint
  instability, nerve impingement; use caution with osteoporosis.

## A Pattern Worth Noticing
Across almost every technique, the same red flags repeat: **acute
inflammation/injury, clotting risk (DVT), fragile bone or skin, and
uncontrolled cardiovascular conditions.** If you learn to recognize those
four categories, you can reason through most contraindication questions
about any specific technique — even ones you haven't memorized.
