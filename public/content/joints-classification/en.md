# Joint Classification

## Two Ways to Classify a Joint
Joints can be described two ways: by their **structure** (what material
connects the bones) or by their **function** (how much they move). These
two systems overlap, and exam questions test both.

## Structural Classification

### 1. Fibrous Joints
Bones are connected directly by dense connective tissue, with no joint
cavity.
- **Example**: sutures between the bones of the skull.
- **Functional classification**: usually **synarthrosis** (immovable).
- **Exception**: the joints between the radius/ulna and between the
  tibia/fibula are structurally fibrous but functionally
  **amphiarthrosis** (slightly movable) — a common trick point on exams.

### 2. Cartilaginous Joints
Bones are connected by cartilage (hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage).
- **Examples**: joints between vertebral bodies (intervertebral discs),
  the pubic symphysis.
- **Functional classification**: **amphiarthrosis** (slightly movable) —
  allows limited motion like spinal bending/twisting.
- A related but functionally immovable version (like the hyaline
  cartilage plate seen in a growing long bone) is classified as
  **synarthrosis**.

### 3. Synovial Joints
Bones are separated by a fluid-filled joint cavity, surrounded by a
joint capsule of ligaments and a synovial membrane; the bone ends are
covered in hyaline cartilage.
- **Functional classification**: **diarthrosis** (freely movable) — this
  is the most mobile joint category, and the type massage therapists
  interact with most often.

## Functional Classification (By Amount of Movement)
- **Synarthrosis**: immovable (most fibrous joints).
- **Amphiarthrosis**: slightly movable (most cartilaginous joints, plus
  the radioulnar/tibiofibular fibrous exceptions).
- **Diarthrosis**: freely movable (all synovial joints).

## The Six Types of Synovial Joints
All are diarthrotic (freely movable), but they differ in *how* they
move based on the shape of the articulating surfaces.

| Type | Shape Description | Movement Allowed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ball-and-socket | Round head fits into a cup-shaped socket | All planes + rotation (most mobile type) | Shoulder, hip |
| Condyloid (ellipsoidal) | Oval condyle fits an elliptical cavity | Flexion/extension, abduction/adduction — no rotation | Wrist (radiocarpal), metacarpophalangeal joints |
| Saddle | Both surfaces are concave/convex ("saddle-shaped") | Multiple planes of movement | Base of the thumb (carpometacarpal joint) |
| Hinge | Convex surface fits a concave surface | Flexion/extension only, one plane | Elbow, knee, interphalangeal joints |
| Pivot | Cylindrical surface rotates within a ring of bone/ligament | Rotation around a single axis | Between the atlas (C1) and axis (C2); proximal radioulnar joint |
| Gliding (plane) | Nearly flat, slightly curved surfaces | Short sliding/twisting movements | Between carpal bones, sacroiliac joint, some rib-sternum joints |

**Which is the most mobile?** The shoulder (a ball-and-socket joint) has
the greatest range of motion of any joint in the body — which is also
why it's the most frequently injured/dislocated major joint.

## Why This Matters for Massage
- Knowing a joint's type tells you what movement to expect (and what's
  abnormal) during range-of-motion assessment.
- Hinge joints (elbow, knee) only need flexion/extension assessed — no
  need to check rotation, since it isn't a normal available motion
  there.
- Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) need the fullest assessment,
  since they move in every plane.
