# Client Assessment & Documentation

## Assessment Is Ongoing, Not Just Intake
A common trap in exam questions is treating "assessment" as something
that only happens at the start of a session. In reality, assessment
continues throughout the session — checking in on pressure, watching for
tissue response, adjusting the plan as needed.

## Client Intake & Health History
Key vocabulary:
- **Subjective data**: what the client reports (how something feels,
  when it started, what makes it better/worse).
- **Objective data**: what the therapist observes or measures directly
  (posture, visible swelling, range of motion, skin condition).
- **Chief complaint**: the main issue the client wants addressed.
- **Red flag**: a finding that suggests something beyond a simple
  musculoskeletal issue — possibly requiring medical referral before (or
  instead of) massage.
- **Medical referral**: sending the client to a physician or other
  licensed provider when something is outside scope or unclear.

The purpose of intake is to determine **whether massage is appropriate
and how it should be modified** — not to diagnose. If a condition is
unclear or seems to be worsening, the safe choice is to pause and refer,
not to guess and proceed.

## Palpation & Observation
Palpation is touch-based assessment, used to gather information — not to
diagnose.

- **Tissue texture, tone, temperature**: baseline qualities felt through
  palpation.
- **Edema**: fluid swelling, felt as puffiness.
- **Guarding**: involuntary muscle tensing in response to pain or
  perceived threat — often makes an area feel "locked up."
- **Symmetry / asymmetry**: comparing left and right sides for
  differences.
- **Hypertonicity**: excessive muscle tone/tension.
- **Hypotonicity**: reduced muscle tone.

Palpation and observation guide *how* to work (pressure, pacing,
technique choice) — they never justify a diagnosis. If something feels
abnormal in a way that concerns you, that's a cue to ask more questions
or refer, not to label it medically.

## Treatment Planning
- **Client-centered goals**: the plan should reflect what the *client*
  wants from the session, not just what the therapist finds
  interesting to work on.
- **Session pacing**: how quickly/slowly the therapist moves through
  areas and techniques.
- **Pressure modification**: adjusting depth of pressure based on
  condition, tolerance, and feedback.
- **Reassessment**: checking in during the session — "how does that
  pressure feel?" — and adjusting.
- **Treatment adaptation**: changing the plan mid-session based on new
  information (a new area of tension found, discomfort reported, etc.).

The core rule: **if the client reports discomfort, the correct response
is to adjust technique or pressure — not to push through it.**

## Documentation: SOAP Notes
SOAP is the standard documentation format:
- **S — Subjective**: what the client reports in their own words.
- **O — Objective**: what the therapist observes or measures.
- **A — Assessment**: the therapist's clinical interpretation, *within
  scope* (e.g., "tissue appears hypertonic," not "client has a herniated
  disc").
- **P — Plan**: what will be done this session and/or next session.

## Documentation Best Practices
- Notes should be **factual and objective** — free of personal opinions
  or diagnostic language.
- The general rule of thumb: *if it wasn't documented, it didn't
  happen* — thorough documentation is a legal safeguard as well as a
  clinical one.
- Records should be kept confidential and retained according to
  applicable regulations.

## Common Exam Scenarios
- A client reports new numbness that wasn't present before: **pause and
  recommend medical referral** — new neurological symptoms are a red
  flag, not something to work through.
- A client reports sharp pain during a technique: **reduce pressure and
  reassess** — don't continue at the same intensity.
- A client asks for advice on changing medication dosage: **stay within
  scope and refer to a licensed medical provider.**
- A client has visible, undiagnosed skin lesions: **avoid the area and
  follow hygiene/infection-control protocols** rather than treating it
  directly.
