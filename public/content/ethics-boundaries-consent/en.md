# Professional Ethics, Boundaries & Consent

## Why the Exam Cares About This
Ethics and law questions on the MBLEx rarely test your personal opinion.
They test whether you can, in order:

1. Keep the client safe
2. Stay within your legal scope of practice
3. Obtain and maintain informed consent
4. Keep professional boundaries intact
5. Act in the client's interest, not your own

When two answer choices both sound reasonable, the one that better protects
the client (and, secondarily, the profession) is almost always the correct
one.

## Core Ethical Principles
- **Autonomy** – the client's right to make their own decisions about their
  body and their care.
- **Beneficence** – acting in ways that benefit the client.
- **Nonmaleficence** – "do no harm"; avoiding actions that could hurt the
  client.
- **Justice** – treating clients fairly and without discrimination.
- **Veracity** – being truthful with clients.
- **Fidelity** – keeping your commitments and being trustworthy.

## Informed Consent
Consent is **not a one-time signature** — it is an ongoing conversation.

- The client must understand and agree to the techniques, areas of the
  body, and pressure being used *before* they happen.
- If anything changes mid-session (new area, deeper pressure, a different
  technique), consent must be re-established.
- The client can withdraw consent at any point, for any reason.
- Documentation should reflect that consent was given and, ideally, that it
  was ongoing.

## Professional Boundaries
A therapeutic relationship has a built-in **power differential**: the
client is often vulnerable (undressed, relaxed, touched), and the
therapist holds professional authority. Because of this imbalance, the
therapist — not the client — is always responsible for maintaining
boundaries.

- **Boundary crossing**: a minor, often harmless departure from the usual
  professional frame (e.g., a session running a few minutes long).
- **Boundary violation**: a serious breach that harms the client or the
  therapeutic relationship (e.g., any sexual conduct, exploiting the
  client financially or emotionally).
- **Transference**: when the client projects feelings (onto the therapist)
  that really belong to another relationship in their life.
- **Countertransference**: when the therapist's own feelings toward the
  client start to influence their professional judgment.
- Romantic or sexual involvement with a client is **always** unethical,
  regardless of who initiates it or whether the client consents.

## Dual Relationships & Gifts
A dual relationship exists when the therapist and client interact in more
than one role — for example, the client is also a neighbor, a business
contact, or a friend.

- Multiple roles create a conflict of interest and can cloud professional
  judgment.
- A useful test: *if a situation benefits the therapist more than the
  client, treat it as an ethical risk.*
- Accepting gifts, discounts, or favors from clients should be approached
  cautiously, since it can blur the professional relationship.

## Scope of Practice
Scope of practice is defined by **law**, not by how much training or
experience a therapist has.

- Massage therapists assess and support the body; they do **not**
  diagnose disease, prescribe treatment, or give medical advice.
- Giving a client instructions to change medication, or telling them what
  disease they have, is outside scope — even if the therapist happens to
  be right.
- When something is outside scope, the correct response is to explain the
  limitation and refer the client to an appropriate licensed provider.

## Mandatory Reporting
In many jurisdictions, therapists are legally required to report specific
situations — most commonly suspected abuse or neglect of a child, elder,
or dependent adult, or an imminent risk of harm.

- Mandatory reporting duties **override client confidentiality** when
  safety is at risk.
- The safest choice on the exam is usually the option that protects a
  vulnerable person from harm, even if it means breaking confidentiality.

## Documentation
- **SOAP notes** are the standard format: Subjective (what the client
  reports), Objective (what the therapist observes/measures), Assessment
  (clinical interpretation within scope), Plan (next steps).
- Documentation should be factual and free of personal opinions or
  diagnostic language.
- The general rule: *if it wasn't written down, it didn't happen* — good
  documentation is also a legal safeguard for the therapist.

## A Simple Decision Order for Ethics Questions
When an exam scenario feels ambiguous, working through these questions in
order usually reveals the best answer:

1. Is the client safe right now? (If not: address safety/reporting first.)
2. Is this within my legal scope of practice?
3. Is informed consent present and current?
4. Are professional boundaries intact?
5. Does this choice benefit the client — not the therapist?

Whichever answer best satisfies all five, in that order, is typically the
credited response.
