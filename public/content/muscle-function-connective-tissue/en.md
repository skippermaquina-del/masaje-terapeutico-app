# Muscle Function & Connective Tissue

## Origin vs. Insertion
- **Origin**: the attachment point that stays relatively fixed/stationary
  during a muscle's contraction. Usually closer to the midline or the
  root of a limb.
- **Insertion**: the attachment point that moves. Usually farther from
  the midline.
- Knowing the *action* of a muscle often helps you reason out which end
  must be the insertion (the end that moves toward the origin when the
  muscle shortens).

## Roles Muscles Play in a Movement
- **Agonist (prime mover)**: the main muscle producing a movement.
  Usually the largest, strongest muscle for that action.
- **Antagonist**: the muscle that produces the opposite movement of the
  agonist, and typically relaxes/lengthens while the agonist contracts.
- **Synergist**: a muscle that assists the agonist by moving the same
  way — if the agonist is concentrically contracting, so is the
  synergist.
- **Fixator (stabilizer)**: a muscle that stabilizes a joint or body part
  so the agonist has a firm base to pull from. Injuries often happen when
  a stabilizer is forced to act as a prime mover (e.g., bending at the
  waist to lift something instead of using the legs).

## Types of Muscle Contraction
- **Isometric**: the muscle generates tension but the joint doesn't move
  (e.g., pressing your palms together). Agonist and antagonist exert
  equal force.
- **Isotonic**: the muscle generates tension *and* the joint moves. Two
  sub-types:
  - **Concentric**: the muscle shortens while contracting (the "lifting"
    or "up" phase of a movement).
  - **Eccentric**: the muscle lengthens while still contracting (the
    "lowering" or "down" phase — this is what lets you set something down
    gently instead of dropping it).

## Proprioception: How the Body Senses Itself
- **Proprioception**: the ability to sense where your body is in space
  without looking (e.g., knowing your arm is raised with your eyes
  closed).
- **Muscle spindles**: stretch receptors inside the muscle. When a muscle
  is stretched too quickly or too far, they trigger a protective
  contraction (the stretch reflex) to prevent overstretching/tearing.
- **Golgi tendon organs (GTOs)**: receptors located in the tendon near
  the muscle-tendon junction. When tension gets too high (e.g., lifting
  something too heavy), GTOs trigger the muscle to relax, protecting the
  tendon and muscle from tearing.
- Certain massage techniques (like rhythmic petrissage) can influence
  these receptors, contributing to the relaxation response of massage.

## Connective Tissues of the Musculoskeletal System
- **Tendon**: connects muscle to bone (e.g., the Achilles tendon connects
  the calf muscles to the heel bone).
- **Ligament**: connects bone to bone, stabilizing joints (e.g., the
  anterior cruciate ligament connects the femur to the tibia). Ligaments
  have a relatively poor blood supply, so they heal slowly.
- **Fascia**: a web-like connective tissue that wraps around and connects
  muscles, organs, and other structures — providing support, protection,
  and acting as a shock absorber. Superficial fascia sits just under the
  skin; deep fascia surrounds individual muscles and muscle groups.
- **Cartilage**: a flexible connective tissue that cushions between
  bones (e.g., the cartilage between the femur and tibia at the knee).
- **Bursa** (plural: bursae): a small, fluid-filled sac (filled with
  synovial fluid) that cushions the space between bone and tendon, or
  bone and muscle, near a joint — reducing friction during movement.

## Why This Vocabulary Matters
Nearly every muscle-action table you'll study is built from these terms:
which muscle is the agonist for a movement, which are its synergists and
antagonists, and whether a given exercise or stretch involves a
concentric, eccentric, or isometric contraction. Getting comfortable with
these concepts first makes memorizing specific muscles much faster.
